1900

Prices as a random process

In Théorie de la spéculation, Bachelier models price change as a continuous process. In modern notation: dSt = μdt + σdWt. The model is a starting point, not a claim that prices are literally Gaussian.

BACHELIER / STOCHASTIC MODEL
Mechanism

Bachelier assumes increments are independent and normally distributed. Over a horizon t, the model implies St − S0 ~ N(μt, σ²t). Variance grows linearly with time, a useful benchmark for testing how observed returns depart from a random walk.

Read the paper
Louis Bachelier, “Théorie de la spéculation” ↗
1973

Risk becomes a replicating portfolio

Black, Scholes, and Merton turn no-arbitrage into a pricing engine. For a European call: C = S0N(d1) − Ke−rTN(d2). The result depends on continuous hedging, frictionless trading, and an estimated volatility.

BLACK–SCHOLES–MERTON / NO-ARBITRAGE
Mechanism

The hedge ratio Δ = ∂C/∂S changes as the underlying moves. Rebalancing the stock position offsets the option's local exposure; the remaining return is tied to the risk-free rate under the model's assumptions.

Read the papers
Black & Scholes, “The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities” ↗ Merton, “Theory of Rational Option Pricing” ↗
1987

When hedging becomes feedback

The October crash made a control loop visible: falling prices can trigger portfolio rebalancing, which creates more selling. Gennotte and Leland formalize how a liquidity shock can be amplified by feedback trading.

1987 CRASH / LIQUIDITY FEEDBACK
Mechanism

A price move changes portfolio weights. If many strategies rebalance in the same direction, aggregate demand becomes a function of the price move itself: Qt = Q0 − αΔPt. With limited depth, that endogenous order flow can magnify the initial shock.

Read the paper
Gennotte & Leland, “Market Liquidity and Stock Market Crashes” ↗
2010

Order flow meets a thin book

During the Flash Crash, a large automated E-mini sale met rapidly changing liquidity. Kyle's microstructure lens writes the first approximation as ΔP = λQ: price response depends on net order flow and market depth.

FLASH CRASH / PRICE IMPACT
Mechanism

Here Q is signed net order flow and λ is inverse market depth. When displayed liquidity is withdrawn, the same quantity must travel through more price levels: effective λ rises, so a fixed order produces a larger move.

Read the evidence
SEC & CFTC, “Findings Regarding the Market Events of May 6, 2010” ↗ Kirilenko et al., “The Flash Crash” ↗

The technical lens

Questions worth asking
before trusting a result.

01

What is the state variable?

Price, volatility, inventory, and order flow are not interchangeable. Define what is observed before explaining what moved.

↗
02

Which assumptions bind?

Continuous trading and zero frictions make a clean equation. Real markets answer with spreads, gaps, queues, and discrete hedges.

↗
03

Where is the feedback?

A strategy is not passive when its trades change the state it reacts to. Close the loop between signal, execution, and impact.

↗
04

What does the evidence identify?

Correlation is not a mechanism. Prefer audit-trail data, stated counterfactuals, and results that survive costs and alternative specifications.

↗

From theory to practice

The point is not to
predict everything.

So what does this
change for a trader?

These models do not make markets easy. They make your questions sharper. Use history to identify what you are actually trading: a distribution, a hedge, a feedback loop, or a liquidity constraint.

01 / BEFORE

Write the mechanism

What should move price, and through which variable? If you cannot name the mechanism, you have a view — not a testable trade.

EXAMPLE: “A volatility shock changes the option’s delta.”
02 / DURING

Measure the state

Track the quantity your model depends on: realized volatility, spread, depth, signed flow, inventory, or time to expiry.

EXAMPLE: “The book is thinner, so λ is probably higher.”
03 / AFTER

Audit the mismatch

Compare the forecast with the path that actually happened. Include fees, slippage, gaps, and the trades you did not take.

EXAMPLE: “The signal worked; execution erased the edge.”
MODEL / TRADING USE
BachelierBuild a baseline distribution. Ask how fat tails, volatility clustering, and jumps invalidate it.
Black–ScholesUse it as a language for sensitivities — delta, gamma, theta, vega — not as a promise of “fair value.”
Liquidity feedbackSize for the exit. A stop is not a guaranteed price when everyone reaches for the door together.
Price impactEstimate whether your order changes the market. A backtest at the mid-price is not an executable result.
A necessary boundary

Learning a model is not the same as finding an edge. Start with paper trading, define maximum loss before entry, and treat every historical result as conditional on its data, costs, and regime.
